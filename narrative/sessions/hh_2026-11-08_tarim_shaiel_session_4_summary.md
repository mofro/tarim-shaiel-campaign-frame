# The Fifteenth Day of the Merchants' Stretch, 1200 HB

---

The cold arrives first — not the travelling kind that meets you on the road, but the settled kind that belongs to old stone buildings: specific, archival, carrying the particular stillness of rooms that have held the same silence for many years. The lamp on the clerk's table, when extinguished, left the smell of its own extinction hanging in the air. It did not spit when it went out.

The city of Puli Khumri receives pilgrims and scholars the way most cities receive caravans — with professional courtesy and limited actual interest. The party arrived having already assembled a small collection of unanswerable questions, and spent the afternoon adding to it.

The clerical woman at the lamp-and-candle temple received the cracked lantern with the composed attention of someone who has learned not to show surprise in front of strangers. She turned it in the firelight. She noted the crack — not struck, not aged. Deliberate. She could not place the workmanship. She said this plainly, and offered something in return: Nadir Khasakam had passed through the city approximately one month prior. Had not stopped. Had been, by her account, moving as though movement itself was urgent. The letter she had received from him before his arrival had asked about places where the lamps for the dead will not stay lit.

She had not known what to make of that then. She said she was beginning to.

She described it as a consumer of the unrecorded. Not hunger in the animal sense. Something with method.

The sword and the tome went to a blacksmith's assessment — practical, honest, unremarkable in its conclusions. The enchanter Tim of the Enchanted Chalice provided rather more. She worked the kind of magic that does not announce itself, the finery-and-gauntlet trade, objects with layers. The party's objects had none. She used the word *pure*, which she seemed to intend as praise and found herself unable to sustain. What she meant was that they were conduits rather than constructs — that whatever power animated them came from somewhere else, expressed through a single uncomplex channel rather than woven from borrowed forces. She did not know the source. She said this as though admitting an embarrassment.

The compass. She was fascinated by the compass.

The compass, presented by Sahir, had given two readings at a fork in the road some days prior. Simultaneously. The enchanter noted that this should not be possible, that navigation instruments were among the most conceptually straightforward magical objects — a single relationship between a single object and a fixed point. What Sahir had described was an instrument receiving two contradictory correct answers at the same instant and holding them without resolution. She spent some time thinking about what that would require. She did not reach a conclusion she was comfortable sharing.

There was also the coin. It had appeared in Rill's pocket after a traveling merchant departed, without having been placed there. When spun, it did not fall. When a true statement was made and the coin flipped, it landed on a consistent face. When a false statement was made, it fell differently. When no statement was made, it spun as normal. When the same test was attempted a second time, the second flip behaved as an ordinary coin.

One accurate answer per day, perhaps. Or one accurate answer per true question, and the threshold for what counts as true is something none of them have yet understood.

The enchanter's final word on all of it was careful: she believed the objects had been given, not found. The word she reached for, and then pulled back from, was *bestowed*.

What Nadir Khasakam was asking about, and where he has gone with the question, remains open.
