## Narrative Summary

The precipice took the wind and replayed it back twice over, cold and thin, tasting of snow that would not fall until winter deigned it.

Ariel stood at the lip of the stone where the mountain gave up it"s gentle slope to sharpness and dizzying height, her wings furled tight against her back. Below her the valley was dying by inches.

Though her vision was clear she could not name the army that moved among the Tulpar and the Human troops like waves. Angry, urgent movements that suggested mindless purpose.  Despite being person shaped, and sized, the mass had no clean edges — a writhing throng of wrongness that  swallowed torchlight rather than reflecting it, that left the screaming behind it strangely delayed, as if sound itself flinched from getting too close. Where it went, lines broke.

The mixed band — Tulpar-shoulders wedged against human shoulders, tusks and iron both — had started the night in a wedge formation, disciplined, holding. Now they were a ragtag ring of sweat and exhaustion shrinking inside a larger ring, herded like cattle who had only just realized the fences were closing. A Tulpar warlord roared something in the old tongue and cut down whatever had come nearest him; three heartbeats later the grasping mob took him from behind, folding him sideways with no weapons Ariel could see, only silence and the crush of many bodies whose force was argument's end.

Her hands closed on nothing. Her jaw set. Bearing witness.

"I must end this," she thought, weight shifting forward, the muscles of her legs already committed to the dive that would carry her down through a thousand feet of screaming air and into the middle of that dying ring, sword first, mercy after.

Raizen's hand found her shoulder before her feet left the stone.
It was not an overly strong grip. He'd never needed to show strength; his hands had always carried something heavier than force, some old patience worn into the calluses, and it stopped her more completely with it's tenderness. She turned on him, wings flaring half open, the question already sharp in her throat — and found no argument waiting in his face. Only sympathy, old and tired and entirely without apology, and beneath it something worse: certainty.

He said nothing. He only shook his head, slowly, the smallest motion, the kind someone makes when the words would cost more than the silence.

 "Not now." 

Below them, the ring grew smaller.
