The liberation of the Tulpar stands as one of the most significant — and most complicated — events in recent Silk Road history. What began as a heroic intervention became a generational tragedy, and then slowly transformed into a story of resilience and adaptation that has taken a thousand years to reach this road, this caravan, this morning.

Before the chains, there was no unified Tulpar people — and the chains did not unify them. Different populations served different masters across the known world: heavy labor and mining in the western empires; seasonal work tending horses for the nomadic powers of the steppes; jade extraction and monumental construction in the east; agricultural work and temple-raising on the subcontinent. The conditions differed. The languages imposed differed. The strategies for surviving differed. What did not differ: the chains. And the knowledge that there had been a time before them.

Throughout captivity, Tulpar maintained oral traditions — songs, remembrances, the memory of a world that hadn't asked them to make themselves invisible. They staged rebellions that were usually crushed. They built covert networks. They preserved fragments. Some earned limited freedoms through service that impressed their captors. Many learned to become invisible in another sense: do the work, avoid notice, survive.

The liberation came through a coalition that acted broadly and simultaneously across the known world. A thousand years of hindsight has not produced a consensus on exactly how or by whom — the Chain Chronicles hold multiple accounts that do not all agree. What they agree on: it worked, it was unequivocally right, and the immediate aftermath killed between a third and a half of the people it freed.

This is not a contradiction. It is the history.

Millions freed without plan, infrastructure, or coordination. Several empires destabilized by the sudden loss of forced labor. Some Tulpar attacked former masters — years of violence with nowhere to go, finally given a direction. Imperial forces attempted recapture. Mass migrations in every direction at once, with no homelands to return to and no infrastructure to receive them. Starvation. Disease. Exposure. The conservative estimates are thirty to forty percent dead in the first five years.

Not failure. Aftermath. The coalition that freed the Tulpar could not have planned for this without planning never to act. The chains were wrong. Breaking them was right. The deaths were real.

Over the following decades, surviving populations coalesced — in mountain passes, river valleys, positions along trade routes where the caravan economy needed guards who could fight from a dozen different traditions. Clans formed around geography, around shared practices, around the work of remembering. The weapon trade emerged: Tulpar merchants who had learned the combat traditions of many masters offered weapons in many styles and knew their use. Guard contracts followed. The academies came later — formal schools of multi-style weapon training, open to non-Tulpar for a fee, which became the first sites of Tulpar culture freely chosen rather than enforced.

The Chain Chronicles were formalized roughly nine hundred years ago. The name comes from the chains — a refusal to forget the origin, and an insistence that what was built after them matters as much as what preceded them. Inter-clan gatherings began within a century. They occur every ten years now.

A thousand years later, Tulpar populations number in the millions, concentrated in trade cities, mountain regions, and the border territories where imperial administration has historically been thin. A merchant class has emerged through trade and craft. Most Tulpar are working people — laborers, guards, craftspeople. The economic marginalization of the chaos period echoes still in some regions; in others it has faded to prejudice without force of law. There is no unified Tulpar culture. The clans are too distinct, the histories too divergent. What bonds them is the liberation narrative: not gratitude for it, not simplification of it, but ownership of it.

There are questions that remain open. The homelands that existed before the captivity — whether they can be identified, reclaimed, or desired. Many clan lineages were severed in the chaos: the knowledge of who came from where and with whom, simply lost. The Remembrancers work at it still.

I have been trading the western roads for six cycles. I have told this history at a hundred campfires to people who did not know it. I will keep telling it. The Chain asks this. The road provides the occasions.
