# The Lower Pantheon

> **Design Principle:** The Celestial Court (Sky-Father, Weighmaster, Conquering Heaven, Jade Illusionist, Memory-Keeper) operates at cosmological scale — structural, remote, rarely petitioned directly. The lower pantheon is where ordinary people actually live their spiritual lives. These are the gods you curse at, bargain with, blame, and thank on a daily basis.
>
> **Three structural types exist in this pantheon:**
> - **Court Emanations** — lower expressions of a Celestial Court power, delegated downward into mortal-scale domains
> - **Threshold Deities** — older or foreign forces that predate (or operate outside) the Court's authority; they exist in the cracks
> - **Cultural Masks** — the same underlying force wearing different names and faces across regions; one entity, multiple valid interpretations
>
> **The same-force principle:** Different cultures worship the same underlying divine forces without knowing it. A Zoroastrian-analog merchant making an offering to the Road-Keeper and a Steppe shaman singing to the Waywalker are addressing the same entity. Neither is wrong. The gods don't correct the taxonomy.

---

## I. CROSS-CULTURAL FORCES (Same deity, many masks)

*These forces cut across all four cultural spheres — Persian/Zoroastrian, Central Asian/Steppe, Tang-Chinese, and Indian/Subcontinent. Each has regional names and faces, but the underlying entity is the same.*

---

### THE ROAD-KEEPER
**Domain:** Travel, safe passage, threshold crossings, caravans
**Structural type:** Court Emanation (from the Weighmaster — travel is accountable; debts incurred on the road are tracked)
**Relationship to players:** Most universally invoked deity on the Silk Road; practically everyone in a caravan context makes some acknowledgment

**Persian/Western face — Rashnu-e-Rah** ("Righteous-of-the-Road")
A tall figure carrying scales and a staff. Not a protector against bandits — that's a different force entirely. Rashnu-e-Rah ensures that what you *deserve* on the road is what you receive. Merchants who cheat suppliers have bad journeys. Travelers who give generously at shrines arrive safely. The accounting is strict, quiet, and never announced in advance. Fire-temple keepers in western cities maintain small road-shrines where travelers report their intentions before departure; the priest records it.

**Steppe/Turkic face — Yol-Tanri** ("Road-Heaven")
Not a person but a presence — the feeling of the road itself becoming aware of you. Nomadic tradition doesn't build shrines to Yol-Tanri because you can't pin a presence to a place. Instead: you speak to the road aloud before you ride onto it. You tell it where you're going, who you're carrying, whether you mean harm to anyone. The road hears. It doesn't respond. But it remembers. Shamans can read the road's memory — tracks, wind patterns, the behavior of horses — and know who traveled safely and who didn't.

**Eastern/Tang face — Tù-Dì Gōng (the Road Aspect)** ("Earth-Road Lord")
In the eastern cultural sphere, road protection falls to a local manifestation of the Earth God, who has a road-specific aspect. He's depicted as a smiling old man with a staff and a small purse. He doesn't judge; he just... keeps track. The shrines are tiny — stone boxes at crossroads, oasis junctions, mountain pass entries. Travelers drop a coin in, state their destination, and bow. He files it. If you never come back, he knows.

**Indian/Subcontinent face — Kshetrapala** ("Guardian of the Field/Road")
In the southern and southeastern sphere, the road guardian is more territorial — he owns the ground you're walking on and is permitting you to cross it. The offering isn't accounting; it's rent. Travelers leave small rice or grain offerings at trail markers. Failing to pay is not just bad luck; it's offense. The Kshetrapala doesn't punish arbitrarily, but withheld rent earns withheld protection. Distinctly: he has no interest in your morality, only in your acknowledgment of his domain.

**GM Notes:**
The Road-Keeper's cross-cultural consistency makes him an ideal "ambient" deity that players encounter everywhere without it being a plot point. His Weighmaster connection gives him subtle relevance to the campaign's cosmological stakes — the Wizard's assault on the Threshold would eventually break the road-accounting system, and travelers would begin dying without cause. Shamans and road-priests who notice this first could be early-arc NPCs.

### THE DEATH-COMPANION
**Domain:** The moment of death, passage of souls, grief, the unburied
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — older than the Court, tolerated rather than incorporated; operates in the Warren-layer interface where the Warrens touch death
**Relationship to players:** Encountered in grief moments, funeral customs, places of mass death, and whenever the Warrens are thin

**Persian/Western face — Sraosha** (later syncretized with Azrael in folk tradition)
A winged figure, swift, neutral in expression. He doesn't escort souls — he *records their departure.* Departure is Sraosha's domain; what happens after is above him. In folk practice: when someone dies, a small lamp is lit immediately. The flame is Sraosha's notification. If the flame goes out before the body is buried, it's a terrible omen — the departure wasn't registered. Soldiers killed far from home and never found? Sraosha doesn't have a record. Their souls wander until a priest performs the notification ritual from a distance, naming them aloud three times into fire.

**Steppe/Turkic face — Erlik's Messenger**
Erlik Khan rules the underworld in Steppe tradition, but he has a mortal-world messenger who collects the dead. The Messenger has no name — naming a death deity invites attention. He's described as a rider on a black horse who appears at the moment of death, not before. He doesn't choose who dies (that's fate); he only receives them. Folk practice: at a deathbed, someone sits facing the door to "hold it open" for the Messenger so he can enter and leave cleanly. To die with the door blocked is to trap the Messenger inside the house, which is catastrophic.

**Eastern/Tang face — Meng Po** (孟婆, Old Lady Meng / the Forgetting Lady)
In the eastern cultural sphere, the Death-Companion is the figure at the boundary who ensures the cycle continues: not by judging, not by escorting, but by *ending.* Meng Po stands at the foot of the Naihe Bridge — the Bridge of No Alternative — in the tenth court of Diyu, the furthest point a soul travels before reincarnation. She is old. She has always been old. She offers every soul a bowl of the Five-Flavored Broth of Oblivion (*míhúntāng*, 迷魂汤), and they drink, and they forget — their previous life, their time in the courts of judgment, everything. Then the cycle recommences.

She doesn't compel. She offers. The bowl is extended; the soul takes it. In a thousand years of practice, almost all of them have. The broth tastes, it is said, of everything they loved most and are about to lose. That is why they drink.

What she is *not*: a jailer, a judge, an escort, a punisher. She predates the court hierarchy around her and tolerates rather than serves it. The ten courts are organized around judgment; Meng Po is organized around continuation. These are not the same thing.

The folk tradition's whispered variant: some souls refuse the cup. They cross the Bridge of No Alternative carrying everything — every memory, every wound, every name. What they become on the other side is not ordinary dead. The eastern scholar-tradition has extensive (suppressed) literature on the taxonomy of refusers. The common thread: they remember, and in remembering, they *persist* in a way the natural order did not intend. Some are grievous. Some are simply present in places they have no right to be. All of them are legible to Meng Po as an error in her accounting, and she does not like errors.

**Indian/Subcontinent face — Yama's Messenger, Chitragupta**
Yama (Death) has an accountant-figure named Chitragupta who records all deeds in a ledger. The interesting asymmetry: Chitragupta is accessible. Yama is not. Petitions reach Yama through Chitragupta. Clever mortals have occasionally successfully argued their cases with Chitragupta directly — not asking for life, but asking for *accurate accounting.* "I did not do what the charge says. Check the record." This version of death has a bureaucratic appeals process that is theoretically accessible to anyone sufficiently clever and persistent.

**GM Notes:**
The Death-Companion's Warren-interface origin makes him genuinely cosmologically significant. The Lich Cadre's necromantic inversion of the Warrens is — from the Death-Companion's perspective — an assault on his filing system. He's not an ally of the heroes exactly, but a being with *interests that align* with the Warrens functioning correctly. An NPC who serves the Death-Companion (in any cultural guise) would be motivated to oppose the Lich Cadre for deeply practical reasons.

The Steppe "don't-name-it" tradition is good texture for a door-blocking scene. The eastern "Meng Po refusers" thread is now developed in full as a Regional Exclusive entry — see **MENG PO — The Forgetting Lady** below. The refuser taxonomy connects directly to the Lich Cadre's undead, the Warrens disruption arc, and potentially to the heroes themselves (who have crossed a boundary carrying everything).

### THE HARVEST-WEIGHT
**Domain:** Agriculture, seasons, adequate rains, hunger, famine, the cyclical return of growth
**Structural type:** Court Emanation (from the Sky-Father — the turning of seasons is the Sky-Father's breath, and the Harvest-Weight is its local expression in soil and seed)
**Relationship to players:** Most important deity for settled farming populations; less relevant to the caravan context but intensely relevant in oasis settlements

**Persian/Western face — Spenta Armaiti** (Holy Devotion/Earth-Mother aspect)
In the Zoroastrian-adjacent tradition, the earth herself is a divine being — Spenta Armaiti — who rewards honest labor with fertility. This is not a personal relationship in the way of a prayer answered. It's a principle: the earth gives back what you put into it, honestly. Cheating the earth (overworking soil, poisoning water) is a form of cosmic offense. Priests of Spenta Armaiti are agricultural engineers as much as religious figures; maintaining irrigation systems is a religious act.

**Steppe/Turkic face — Umay Ana** ("Mother Umay")
The protective earth-goddess of the steppe tradition, associated with fertility, children, and the preservation of life through difficult seasons. Unlike the Persian version, Umay Ana is personal — she has preferences, moods, and can be offended or pleased. Nomadic families carry small felt figures (umay-idols) representing her presence in the home. When a child is born healthy, the mother thanks Umay Ana. When crops fail (on settled Steppe communities), the failure is understood as Umay Ana withdrawing protection, usually because of some community transgression.

**Eastern/Tang face — Shennong's Descendants**
The eastern tradition anthropologizes agriculture into a legendary emperor-figure (the Divine Farmer) who discovered all useful plants. His "descendants" are the local earth-gods who continue his work in specific territories. In this framework, agricultural failure is bureaucratic — the local earth-god wasn't properly petitioned, or the petition was insufficient, or a neighboring territory's earth-god was given better offerings. The system is competitive and administrative, not personal.

**Indian/Subcontinent face — Prithvi / Bhumi**
The earth as goddess in her own right — patient, enormous, capable of both sustenance and catastrophe. The Indian subcontinent tradition uniquely emphasizes *the earth's tolerance*: she endures enormous weight (mountains, cities, the sins of humans) with infinite patience. But when her patience is exceeded, the consequences are geological. Earthquakes are her shifting position. Droughts are her turning away. You cannot exhaust her, but you can lose her attention.

**GM Notes:**
The Harvest-Weight is the deity that makes famine and agricultural crises legible as *spiritual* events in the world. A drought isn't just a weather problem — it's a disruption in the Sky-Father's seasonal breath, filtered through whatever local name the Harvest-Weight wears. This gives village-level NPC motivations real texture: they're not just "worried about food," they're navigating a relationship with a divine force that appears to have withdrawn.

### THE SHAME-KEEPER
**Domain:** Shame, honor, public reputation, social transgression, conscience
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — not a Court emanation; predates the Court's organizational structure; the Shame-Keeper is older than ethics-as-system
**Relationship to players:** Appears in social contexts, moments of moral choice, and any scene where reputation is at stake

**Persian/Western face — the Daena**
In Zoroastrian tradition, the daena is not exactly a deity — it's your own conscience personified, and it meets you at death as either a beautiful or hideous figure. But in folk practice, the Daena is sometimes addressed while living, in moments of private shame. You speak to the image of yourself-as-judge. There are no priests for this; it's entirely internal. What's culturally significant: in this tradition, *you* are the Shame-Keeper, and what you see in yourself is what the universe sees.

**Steppe/Turkic face — Utku** ("Shame-Bearer")
A spirit described as small, dog-sized, and invisible until you have done something shameful — at which point it attaches to you and follows. Everyone can see it even though only you can feel it. This is folk tradition at its most practical: social enforcement through supernatural threat. An elder who says "I see your Utku" is making a specific accusation. A shaman who can "remove an Utku" is performing a genuine community service for someone who has made adequate restitution. Utku that aren't removed follow you into the afterlife.

**Eastern/Tang face — Mian Zi and the Ghost of Lost Face**
In the eastern cultural sphere, shame is collectivized — losing face (mianzi) harms not just you but your family and lineage. The "Ghost of Lost Face" isn't a specific deity but a category of spirit that attaches to families who have suffered public disgrace without restoration. Persistent family misfortune is attributed to this ghost. Restoration requires public acknowledgment, formal apology, and a period of demonstrated recovery. The ghost leaves when the family's social standing is genuinely restored — it's not ritual, it's result.

**Indian/Subcontinent face — Nirrti** (Goddess of Ruin and Misfortune)
Nirrti is specifically the deity of degradation — not just shame, but the particular shame that comes from spiritual failure, ritual transgression, or caste violation. She's depicted as dark, garland of skulls, dwelling in the south (inauspicious direction). She doesn't judge; she simply *arrives* when someone has left themselves spiritually exposed through transgression. Propitiation is elaborate because the offense is serious. But she can be propitiated — she's not permanent unless you refuse to address the transgression.

**GM Notes:**
The Shame-Keeper is the deity most relevant to the heroes' internal arcs. The self-doubt theme runs directly through this territory. A Warrior who questions the morality of past victories, encountering an NPC who speaks of the Shame-Keeper (in any cultural idiom), will feel this land. The Daena version is particularly elegant: the heroes' shame is, in this framework, themselves. They carry it as an internal conversation. This doesn't require a mechanical hook — it's atmosphere.

### THE CHANCE-WEAVER
**Domain:** Luck, fortune, gambling, risk, the pivot moment
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — not a Court emanation; operates outside both the Weighmaster's accounting and the Sky-Father's seasonal order; Chance is, by definition, uncountable
**Relationship to players:** Found in gambling dens, at crossroads, in moments of desperate risk; universally acknowledged and universally mistrusted

**Persian/Western face — Khvarenah** (the Divine Radiance/Fortune)
In the Persian tradition, fortune is not random — it's a divine quality that *rests on* worthy individuals like a visible light. Khvarenah can be lost through unworthiness and regained through righteous action. The sense that a great person has Khvarenah is literal: they seem to glow slightly. Folk practice involves elaborate rituals to attract and maintain Khvarenah, and to protect it from rivals who might steal or diminish it. Merchants attribute business success to maintained Khvarenah; military commanders attribute victories to it.

**Steppe/Turkic face — Irrevocable Fate (Yazgı)**
In the Steppe tradition, what Western cultures call "luck" is actually predetermined fate (yazgı) that cannot be changed — only understood. Shamans can read your fate in signs; knowing it doesn't change it. What looks like luck is just your fate arriving early or late. This tradition is distinctly unconcerned with attracting good fortune because fortune isn't a variable. The practical upshot: Steppe peoples are unusually calm about catastrophic outcomes — "it was fate" is not resignation, it's cosmological clarity.

**Eastern/Tang face — Caishen** (God of Wealth/Fortune)
The eastern god of fortune is frankly mercantile — he deals in wealth as a specific subset of luck. Offerings to Caishen are transactional: you give to receive. His temples are found in every market quarter; merchants light incense before major deals. He's depicted riding a black tiger, holding a golden ingot. Unlike the Persian version, Caishen's favor is available to anyone who makes proper offerings — worthiness is not required, only proper commercial practice. This makes him one of the most widely worshipped deities in cosmopolitan hubs where different traditions mix.

**Indian/Subcontinent face — Lakshmi and Alakshmi**
The Indian tradition uniquely doubles this deity: Lakshmi is fortune and abundance; her sister Alakshmi is misfortune and ruin. They are inseparable — where Lakshmi enters a door, Alakshmi may follow. The practice is therefore as much about *keeping Alakshmi out* as attracting Lakshmi in. Thresholds are ritually protected; certain actions (specific foods, behaviors, visitors) are associated with Alakshmi's presence. The pairing is theologically honest in a way other traditions avoid: fortune and misfortune are the same force with two faces.

**GM Notes:**
The Chance-Weaver is the deity gamblers invoke, caravan leaders pray to before a risky route, and soldiers acknowledge before battle. His Threshold-Deity status (operating outside the Weighmaster's accounting) is cosmologically interesting — Chance is the one thing that can't be predicted by the Celestial ledger. The Wizard's long-term plan succeeds partly because he engineered outcomes that *look like chance* but aren't. An NPC devoted to the Chance-Weaver who notices that "luck has been wrong lately" could be an early-arc signal that something is being manipulated.

## II. REGIONAL EXCLUSIVES

*These deities belong specifically to one cultural sphere. They don't have equivalents elsewhere — or if they do, the equivalent is sufficiently different that it's a distinct entity rather than a different mask.*

---

### ANAHITA — The River-Queen (Persian/Zoroastrian exclusive)
**Domain:** Rivers, water, fertility through water, abundance, purification
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — old Iranian water-goddess, predates Zoroastrian systematization; incorporated but never fully subordinated

**Character:** Anahita is depicted as a young woman in gold armor standing in a river. She is simultaneously the river itself and the deity of the river — the distinction doesn't matter in her tradition. Her waters are sacred: bathing in a river consecrated to Anahita is purification; drinking from it is covenant. Merchants traveling to and from Persia bring small votive figures of her; trading cities along major rivers maintain her temples.

**What she asks:** Anahita doesn't request prayer. She requires *not fouling her water.* Ritual pollution of rivers (dumping refuse, burying bodies upstream of cities, channeling drainage into sacred rivers) is a religious offense, not just an environmental one. Her priests are among the most politically active in the western cultural sphere because their deity's mandate is enforceable: the river can be inspected, violations can be documented, and Anahita's judgment (plague, drought, flooding) is legible to everyone downstream.

**NPC application:** A city in the western cultural sphere that has been dumping industrial waste or battlefield dead into an Anahita-consecrated river will have a population that understands exactly why they're getting sick. The priests know what's happening cosmologically. They're not wrong. This gives environmental disasters a religious dimension that makes NPC politics far more complicated than a simple "clean up the river" quest.

### TENGRI — The Sky Itself (Steppe/Turkic exclusive)
**Domain:** Sky, weather, cosmic order, the eternal, fate at its grandest scale
**Structural type:** Ambiguous — possibly a regional emanation of the Sky-Father, possibly a parallel independent entity; Steppe shamans don't care about the distinction and would find the question offensive

**Character:** Tengri is not depicted. Depicting Tengri is theologically incoherent — you cannot represent the sky in a painting of the sky. The Sky is Tengri. The eternal blue heaven (Kök Tengri) is simultaneously the sky you look at and the divine order that holds everything in place. There are no Tengri temples because you would build the temple *inside* Tengri, which is correct. Worship happens in open air, always, on high ground when possible. The Steppe peoples carry their sacred space with them because their sacred space is *above them, always.*

**What this means in practice:** Orcs (whose mythic foundation draws from Tengri tradition) have a fundamentally different relationship to sacred space than every other culture in the world. Their sacred sites are always open-air — sky altars, ancestor barrows, victory monuments — never enclosed. The idea of worshipping inside a building is not just foreign to them; it's epistemologically wrong. You can't reach Tengri through a roof.

**The Sky-Father question:** The Celestial Court includes a "Sky-Father" figure. Is this Tengri? Steppe shamans who understand the Court's structure would say: "The Sky-Father is not Tengri. The Sky-Father is what Tengri chooses to delegate." Whether this is theologically accurate or a rationalization that preserves Tengri's absolute primacy is something even the shamans debate.

**GM Notes:** Tengri is the deity whose cosmological position is most relevant to the campaign's actual stakes. Hero Heaven (Üst, the Upper World) is, in Steppe cosmology, *inside* Tengri. The heroes having been expelled from Hero Heaven is, in this framework, being removed from the interior of the divine order itself. Steppe shamans who understand this would recognize the heroes not just as returned champions but as beings who have been *cosmologically dislocated from their proper position within Tengri's structure.* That's a different kind of reverence — less "worship" and more "cosmological concern."

### GUANYU — The God of War and Righteousness (Eastern/Tang exclusive)
**Domain:** War, righteous battle, brotherhood, oaths, merchant protection (unexpected dual domain)
**Structural type:** Court Emanation (from the Conquering Heaven, but specifically the aspect of the Conquering Heaven that cares about *how* battles are fought, not just whether they're won)

**Character:** Guanyu is depicted as a massive red-faced general with a long beard, carrying a glaive (the Green Dragon Crescent Blade). He is simultaneously the god of war and the god of sworn brotherhood — the two domains are connected because in the eastern tradition, righteous war is war fought under oath with brothers at your side. Merchants adopted him as a protector because their trade relationships are formalized through oaths, and Guanyu enforces oaths.

**His specific power:** He enforces sworn commitments. If you swear an oath in Guanyu's name and break it, he notices. Not in a lightning-bolt way — in a "your reputation will now fail in every context where you need it, until the oath is discharged or formally dissolved before his altar" way. Eastern merchants will not enter business partnerships without invoking Guanyu; it's the equivalent of a legally binding contract.

**The warrior dimension:** In military contexts, Guanyu is the patron of soldiers who fight with honor. He doesn't protect soldiers who fight well — he protects soldiers who fight *correctly.* Betraying an ally on the battlefield, striking the defenseless, accepting payment to lose — these are Guanyu violations. The punishment is less supernatural and more social: other soldiers will know. Guanyu makes the violation visible.

**NPC application:** Eastern-origin mercenaries and soldiers will invoke Guanyu before battle and hold their commitments very seriously. An NPC from this tradition who is asked to break an oath they made under Guanyu will explain, matter-of-factly, that this is not a question of desire — they literally cannot, not without formal dissolution. This creates clean obstacles and interesting negotiation scenes.

### ANAHIT — The Mountain-Bride (not to be confused with Anahita; a distinct local figure)
**Domain:** High places, mountain shrines, the specific danger of beautiful things, desire that destroys
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — particularly relevant to the Hindu Kush and Pamir approaches; possibly connected to Persian Pari tradition; possibly something older

**Character:** In the mountain-pass cultures between the Persian and Indian cultural spheres, there is a female deity of high places who is uniformly described as: beautiful beyond endurance, impossible to reach, and catastrophically dangerous to those who try. She doesn't want worshippers. She doesn't want offerings. She wants *no attention at all.* The shrines at mountain passes are not dedicated *to* her — they're apotropaic, asking her to look elsewhere while travelers pass.

**Her nature:** The theological puzzle: why is a deity of high places dangerous? Because in this tradition, beauty and danger are the same force — the mountain is beautiful and will kill you; Anahit is beautiful and will kill you; the desire for transcendence is beautiful and will kill you. This deity is not malevolent. She is simply what happens when mortal desire overreaches. Travelers who take the mountain passes safely are travelers who asked only for safe passage and looked at their feet.

**Regional texture:** Local mountain-pass villages have extremely specific folk traditions about Anahit — which side of the pass she sleeps on (the approach side, always — so you don't wake her entering), what times of day are safest, which weather conditions indicate she's paying attention. The folklore is detailed and extremely practical. None of it is consistent from village to village. All of it is taken seriously.

**NPC application:** In the Hindu Kush passes toward Kabul, players will encounter this deity in ambient form — the folk traditions, the apotropaic shrines, the mountain-pass villagers who are intensely superstitious about specific behaviors in high places. An NPC from this tradition would be deeply uncomfortable with any hero who seemed to be attracting *attention* (unusual abilities, visible power, obvious divine favor) in a mountain environment. "Stop shining. You're going to wake her."

### CHITRAGUPTA — The Accountant of Deeds (Indian/Subcontinent exclusive)
**Domain:** Record-keeping, the audit of a life, appeals to divine judgment, cosmic bureaucracy at the mortal interface
**Structural type:** Court Emanation (from the Weighmaster — Chitragupta is, in the Indian tradition, essentially the Weighmaster's subcontractor for the subcontinent)

**Character:** Depicted as a figure with a large ledger, writing constantly. His writing never stops — every deed, every word, every intention is recorded simultaneously with its occurrence. He is not Yama (Death/Judgment). He is Yama's *accountant.* Yama decides; Chitragupta provides the data.

**The accessible appeals process:** Unlike most divine figures, Chitragupta can be petitioned *while alive.* Not to change the record — the record is accurate. But to ensure accuracy. "I am accused of doing X. The record will show that I did not — check it." Priests of Chitragupta serve a quasi-legal function in the Indian cultural sphere: they facilitate appeals to divine record-keeping when human justice has failed. A person who has been falsely accused of something serious may request a Chitragupta audit — a ceremony in which the priest mediates a petition for divine record verification.

**The uncomfortable reality:** Chitragupta audits are binding on the petitioner, not just the accusation. If you petition Chitragupta to verify your innocence of Accusation X, his ledger will be opened for the review — and it contains *everything.* Petitioners sometimes discover that while they were innocent of the specific charge, the opened ledger reveals other things they'd forgotten or hoped no one noticed.

**NPC application:** In the Indian-adjacent cultural sphere (the subcontinent approaches, the mountain-pass communities with Indian trade contact), a character who wants divine certification of their innocence or righteousness has a specific avenue — with specific risks. The heroes, having lived entire lives (and a thousand years of... whatever Hero Heaven was), have extremely long ledgers. A Chitragupta priest might look at one of them and become very, very still.

### VATA / VAYU — The Wind-Lord (cross-Zoroastrian and Vedic, regional to the mountain-pass transition zone)
**Domain:** Wind, breath, the in-between, messages carried over distance, the thing that moves between places
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — exists specifically in the transitional geography between Persian and Indian cultural spheres; winds move through mountain passes and are the literal medium through which these cultures have touched for millennia

**Character:** In Persian tradition: Vayu is ambiguous — both beneficial (life-breath, divine winds) and malevolent (death-wind, the blast that brings plague). In Vedic tradition: Vayu is the breath of the gods, father of Hanuman (the great Simiah hero), pure vital force. In the mountain-pass transition zone between these traditions: a syncretic figure who has absorbed both, and is therefore *genuinely unpredictable.* The same wind that carries trade goods also carries illness. The wind doesn't choose; the wind simply moves.

**The Simiah connection:** Vayu's role as father of Hanuman in the Vedic tradition gives the Simiah (Vanara-analog) people a specific religious relationship to the wind — they are children of the Wind-Lord in a genealogical sense. Simiah universities in the Hindu Kush region maintain wind-observation towers that are simultaneously weather-monitoring infrastructure and Vayu temples. The monks who operate them are both meteorologists and priests.

**NPC application:** In the mountain passes southeast of Balkh, traveling toward Kabul, wind behavior is read as Vayu communication. A favorable wind at the right moment is not just lucky — it's meaningful. An NPC from the mountain-pass culture will interpret wind signs with the same seriousness that a Western character might interpret omens. And they're not wrong to: if the Simiah universities are nearby and have been conducting observational work, there may be *genuine* correlation between Vayu's behavior and cosmological events in the Warrens.

### MENG PO — The Forgetting Lady (Eastern/Tang exclusive)
**Domain:** Forgetting, the threshold before reincarnation, the mercy of the cycle, the taxonomy of those who refuse
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity — older than the Celestial Court's organizational hierarchy; she predates the ten courts of Diyu and would continue her work if the courts dissolved tomorrow; tolerated and structurally necessary, never subordinated

**Character:** Meng Po (孟婆) does not resemble what you might expect. She is depicted as an old woman — always old, never young, though some folk traditions claim she was once a human scholar named Meng Jiang who could not reincarnate because grief over her husband's death held her too tightly to the world. When she finally released him, she released herself too — but she had been waiting so long at the threshold that she simply... stayed. The court hierarchy formalized around her. She predates the formalization.

She stands at the foot of the Naihe Bridge — the Bridge of No Alternative, 奈何桥 — where the tenth court of the dead delivers each soul before it crosses into the next life. The broth she carries (*míhúntāng*, 迷魂汤; also called the Five-Flavored Tea of Forgetfulness) is made from herbs gathered from still water. It smells like nothing. It tastes, the stories say, like everything you loved and are about to lose.

She extends it. The soul takes it. Almost all of them always have.

**What she is not:** A judge. A punisher. An escort. These roles belong to the ten courts, to Yama (Yanluo Wang), to Chitragupta in the Indian-adjacent tradition. Meng Po's function is entirely about *after* judgment — she is what happens to a soul once it has been weighed, assigned, and sent forward. She ensures that the weight it carried in its past life does not corrupt the new one. In this sense her work is merciful, however indifferent she appears. She is not indifferent to the cycle. She is indifferent to individual suffering within it.

**The folk tradition's Lady Meng Jiang variant:** In some regions, Meng Po is explicitly identified with the legendary Lady Meng Jiang, whose husband was conscripted to build the Great Wall and died there. She wept so bitterly at the wall that it collapsed to reveal his bones. Unable to reincarnate because her grief was too total, she eventually made the soup — originally, folk tradition holds, for herself. Then for others. The identification matters narratively: it means Meng Po is not a natural force but a *person who became a function*. She knows what it costs to remember. She offers the forgetting anyway. This is either wisdom or tragedy depending on who is telling the story.

**The refuser taxonomy:** Souls who refuse the cup cross the Bridge of No Alternative carrying everything — their previous lives, their griefs, their names, the full ledger of who they were. What they become is not classified by the court hierarchy because the court hierarchy cannot process them: they are not ordinary dead, not properly living, and they persist in ways the natural order did not design. The eastern scholar-tradition has an extensive (suppressed) literature on the taxonomy of refusers, written by mortal scholars who encountered them and survived long enough to write it down.

Broad categories from that literature:
- **The Anchored** — refusers who remain near a specific place or person from their previous life; not aggressive, but their presence distorts the natural order around them in ways that accumulate over time
- **The Scholars** — refusers who crossed carrying knowledge they considered too important to lose; some are benign, acting as repositories for information that the living cannot otherwise access; some are actively destabilizing, unable to accept that the world moved on without them
- **The Avengers** — refusers who crossed carrying a specific unresolved wrong; the most dangerous category; they are not irrational, but they are *singular*, and they cannot be reasoned out of their purpose
- **The Dreaming** — refusers who crossed carrying so much that they cannot organize it; they persist as atmospheric presences, not quite coherent; shamans and death-priests sometimes describe them as "the ones Meng Po is still waiting for"

Meng Po is aware of every refuser. She keeps her own accounting — a list she carries beneath the bridge where it stays dry. She does not pursue refusers. She waits. Eventually, the literature agrees, they all come back. They always find that the bridge is still there, and she is still at the foot of it, and the bowl is still full.

**What she asks:** She asks for one thing from the living: *don't send her unnecessary work.* Necromantic practice that reanimates souls who have already drunk the broth and been assigned to new lives is a desecration of her process — it unpicks the work she did, re-opens files she closed, and corrupts the natural order she maintains. The Lich Cadre's necromancy is, from Meng Po's perspective, a systematic vandalism of her accounting. She has not done anything about it yet. The literature is not clear on what it would look like if she did.

**NPC application:** In the eastern cultural sphere, Meng Po has no temples — you don't build a temple to the threshold, you encounter it. But there are practitioners: death-priests who serve as intermediaries between the living and the recently dead during the liminal period before a soul crosses, and whose secondary function is to identify refusers and document them. An NPC who is a death-priest in a city with an active Lich Cadre presence would be experiencing an unprecedented professional crisis: souls that should have crossed aren't crossing, refusers are proliferating in uncharacteristic ways, and Meng Po's accounting is developing gaps that the priest can sense but not explain. They would be desperate for someone to tell them *why*.

**The heroes' situation:** This is where it gets delicate. The heroes crossed a threshold — from whatever Hero Heaven was, back into the world. They are carrying everything. They drank nothing. By any definition in the Meng Po tradition, they are refusers — not in the sense of having refused at the bridge, but in the structural sense: they are souls who have persisted across a boundary carrying the full weight of their previous existence. A death-priest who understood this would not know what category to file them under. The Anchored? The Scholars? None of the existing taxonomy was written with the heroes in mind. Meng Po's accounting has a category for them, presumably. The question is what it says.

**GM Notes:** Meng Po is not a confrontable entity — she is a function of the cosmos, not a character in the story. But she can be *encountered* obliquely: through her death-priests, through the refusers themselves (the Lich Cadre's undead may include people who were refusers before the Cadre found them), through the suppressed scholarly literature (a copy of the refuser taxonomy is exactly the kind of thing a Scholar-refuser might have been trying to preserve), and through the heroes' own dawning recognition of what they are. If a death-priest looks at one of the heroes and grows very still, the players will feel it. They don't need to understand why. Yet.

## III. MINOR DEITIES AND SPIRIT POWERS

*Deities of narrower domains — petitioned for specific needs, present in specific contexts, less cosmologically significant but intensely practically relevant.*

---

### THE BATTLE-VOICE
**Domain:** The moment before a battle cry, the decision to commit to violence, the threshold of killing
**Regional home:** All martial cultures; most articulated in Orcish tradition
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity; not a Court emanation; violence is older than the Court's order

The Battle-Voice doesn't want worship. He wants acknowledgment — the beat of recognition when a warrior understands they're about to cross from "might fight" to "will fight." The Orcish Chain Chronicles record him as a god who "speaks once and is done with you" — a single moment of clarity before combat, not protection during it, not victory, just the gift of knowing what you're choosing.

---

### THE REMEMBRANCER-WHO-FORGETS
**Domain:** The tragedy of forgetting, lost names, cultural erasure, the danger of broken oral tradition
**Regional home:** Orcish Confederations, who experienced deliberate cultural erasure under the Empire
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity; possibly a manifestation of the Memory-Keeper's shadow-aspect

Described as an elder who has forgotten their own name. Orc Remembrancers perform an annual ritual acknowledging this deity — not to worship, but to remember *what happens when remembering fails.* The ritual ends with every participant stating their full name, their family's name, and the name of their homeland aloud. The deity is invoked only as a warning.

**Campaign relevance:** The Scholars' Purge was, in terms this deity would understand, a deliberate feeding of souls to the Remembrancer-Who-Forgets. Scholars' knowledge was destroyed, their names erased from records, their works burned. The heroes chasing the sources of that Purge are, in Orcish cosmological terms, undoing a divine desecration.

---

### THE SILK-SPIDER
**Domain:** Trade routes, the weaving of connections, contracts, the making and breaking of networks
**Regional home:** Goblin and Halfling diaspora communities; also merchant guilds of all ancestries
**Structural type:** Court Emanation (from the Weighmaster — trade is accountable, contracts are ledger entries)

Not depicted as a spider — depicted as a weaver with too many hands. The Silk-Spider doesn't bless individual trades; she blesses *networks.* Offerings are made not before a single transaction but at the establishment of a new trade relationship. Her temple in Samarkand is the longest-operating business registry in the world; every partnership registered there has a small offering to the Silk-Spider on file. The idea: she is a passive guarantor, present in every registered agreement.

---

### THE DOOR-WARDEN
**Domain:** Thresholds, doors, entrances, the moment of leaving and arriving, household protection
**Regional home:** Pan-regional but most articulated in eastern and Steppe traditions
**Structural type:** Threshold Deity (literalized — he is the threshold)

The Door-Warden is not a deity you petition for great things. He's the deity you acknowledge on the way in and out of your home every day. A small carved post beside the door, or a painted face above the lintel, or a simple formula spoken when crossing a threshold — these are all Door-Warden acknowledgments. He doesn't provide protection; he provides *noticing.* The Door-Warden witnesses what enters and leaves. If something harmful comes into your home without being stopped, it's because you didn't ask him to notice.

**Campaign use:** Orcish caravanserai culture maintains Door-Warden acknowledgment at every caravanserai entrance. A hero familiar with this tradition who arrives at a caravanserai and notices the Door-Warden acknowledgment has been defaced or omitted would correctly understand: this place is not safe in a specific, spiritual way. Something came in through this door without being witnessed.

---

### THE FEVER-BREAKER
**Domain:** Illness, the crisis point of disease, the moment a fever either breaks or kills
**Regional home:** All regions; most practically articulated in Steppe shamanic tradition
**Structural type:** Ambiguous — possibly connected to the Death-Companion, but operating at the threshold *before* death rather than after

The Fever-Breaker is not a healer deity. Healer deities grant wellness. The Fever-Breaker is present only at the moment of crisis — the night when a fever either peaks and breaks, or kills. Shamans performing illness rituals are not healing the patient; they're negotiating with the Fever-Breaker about which way the crisis will go. The metaphor in Steppe tradition: the Fever-Breaker holds two doors open. Your job is to walk through the right one.

## IV. COSMOLOGICAL NOTES FOR GMs

### How the Lower Pantheon Relates to the Campaign's Stakes

The Wizard's assault on the Threshold, if successful, would systematically break the lower pantheon's infrastructure before it breaks the Celestial Court. The order of damage:

1. **Road-Keeper disruption first** — Caravans begin failing without apparent cause; the road-accounting system breaks down; merchants start dying on routes they've traveled safely for decades
2. **Death-Companion disruption second** — The Warren-interface failure creates a backlog; souls aren't departing properly; the Jiangshi problem becomes epidemic; Albast proliferate because the Albast feed on unanchored souls
3. **Harvest-Weight disruption third** — The Sky-Father's seasonal breath, filtered through the Harvest-Weight, requires Warren-layer circulation; if the Warrens are inverted, seasons become erratic; harvests fail not through drought but through wrong-ness
4. **Chance-Weaver disruption fourth** — Outcomes stop making sense; outcomes that should be random become predictable (the Wizard is engineering them); Khvarenah starts resting on the wrong people

The heroes, if they're paying attention, will notice these symptoms before they understand the cause. NPCs who serve these deities will be among the first to know something is wrong — and the last to understand why.

### Deity-NPC Archetypes for the Balkh → Kabul Stretch

Specifically relevant to the current campaign corridor:

- **Rashnu-e-Rah priest at Balkh** — Road-keeping records; knows who traveled safely and who didn't; notices the recent failures in his accounting system
- **Steppe shaman reading Yol-Tanri's memory** — Can read the road's recent history; has noticed that several travelers who should have arrived safely didn't, and the road has no memory of why
- **Chitragupta priest in a Kabul-adjacent community** — Serves a small Indian diaspora merchant community; has performed audits that revealed things they weren't supposed to find
- **Mountain-pass Anahit-keeper** — Local elder in the Hindu Kush approaches who reads weather and wind as divine communication; deeply uncomfortable with the heroes if they're visible as powerful
- **Simiah wind-monk at Vayu tower** — Observes winds coming through the passes; has noticed that the winds have been *wrong* for three months; not randomly wrong — systematically wrong in a way that suggests someone is managing them
