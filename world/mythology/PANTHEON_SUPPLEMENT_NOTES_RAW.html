I. Structural Clarifications

The Chance-Weaver Problem

The Chance-Weaver needs to go, or more accurately: it never needed to be a separate deity in the first place. Here's the actual distinction that makes this clear.

A trickster figure is an agent. They have intent. The chaos they introduce is authored — Loki chose to do the thing, Coyote wanted to see what would happen, Anansi is running a scheme. When a trickster disrupts your life, there's a relationship happening. You're being messed with. That's fundamentally different from the experience of chance, which is just the unpredictable substrate — pure stochasticity, no author, no intention, no one on the other end of it.

The Jade Illusionist is already the trickster. What we don't need is a separate deity for "random chance as a force" because that's either redundant with the Illusionist or it's not a deity at all — it's just weather.

What's interesting is that regional luck-deities like Khvarenah (Persian) and Caishen (eastern) can now be understood as the Jade Illusionist wearing culturally legible masks. This is actually richer theologically: the Illusionist is operating at Celestial level but has chosen to be available to mortal petitions, and different cultures have developed different interface conventions for reaching it. A Persian merchant invoking Khvarenah and an eastern merchant invoking Caishen are, functionally, calling the same number with different dialing codes.

The Jade Illusionist absolutely does not correct them. Why would it? The confusion is probably interesting.

II. Death as Chorus — Full Design

Why Death Can't Be One Person

The simplest cosmological argument for a death chorus: death has to be present everywhere simultaneously. A single deity of death is a conceptual convenience that doesn't survive any scrutiny — you'd need to explain the logistics, and they don't work. The chorus model resolves this and, conveniently, is also how multiple real-world traditions have handled it.

There's also something richer happening here. A single death deity gives you a personality and a relationship. A chorus gives you a bureaucracy, which is more interesting — there's a hierarchy, there are disputes, there are wrong calls that get appealed. And different cultures can name different members of the same chorus, which is exactly the kind of elegant multicultural convergence this setting is built on.

The Ölüm Sürüsü

The Steppe shamans call them the "Death-Flock" or "Death-Herd" — not one rider but a jurisdiction company. They have named members, which is cosmologically significant: a named thing can be invoked, can be asked questions, can be read. A Steppe shaman doesn't say the generic "Death is coming" when they read the signs. They say "I see Kızıl Kanat circling" — which is specific, actionable information. There's a kind of dying that's approaching. That's useful in a way that "death" isn't.

  • Kara Yel ("Black Wind") is the road that kills. Exposure, cold, storm, the journey that didn't end well. This is the one travelers fear and the one you invoke when you're about to cross a dangerous pass in winter.
  • Kızıl Kanat ("Red Wing") is battle-death. Soldiers invoke this one before combat — not asking for mercy, exactly, but asking for the acknowledgment that comes with dying in a particular context.
  • Tatlı Uyku ("Sweet Sleep") is the one everyone hopes for. Old age, natural end, the quiet version. The most benevolent member. People make small offerings to this one when an elderly person is ill — not to hasten anything, just to make a preference known.
  • Susuz ("The Thirsty") is drought-death, famine, dehydration. Naming it too casually is considered bad luck in desert communities. This is the one the water-carriers' guilds propitiate.
  • Gece Gölgesi ("Night Shadow") is sudden death. No warning, no announcement. By definition, if you're seeing Night Shadow, you're already out of time.

The Crow Court — Wūyā Tǐng (烏鴉廷)

Worth being transparent about the construction here. The components are real: crows as death-omens is documented Chinese tradition (the Sānzúwū, the three-legged crow). The Yama bureaucracy is genuine Chinese folk religion. The individual messenger assigned at birth is more Indian (Yamaduta) than Chinese. The "deliberating flock" interpretation — the specific grammar of one crow versus two versus a flock — that's invented for this world. Use it freely; there's no source material it contradicts.

The visual is the thing to hold onto: a murder of crows landing on a rooftop is the Crow Court in conference. They're comparing notes. Making sure the accounting is correct before anything proceeds. A single crow means the appointment has been made — it's been decided. A flock means still deliberating — you have time to make arrangements.

This is silent GM signaling that, once the players understand the grammar, will make their hair stand up in ways that no dramatic announcement ever could.

The Persian Face — Your Shadow-Member

In Persian folk tradition, your assigned chorus-member has followed you since birth. They are not hostile. They're not waiting to take you — they're documenting, watching, keeping the record that will eventually matter. There's an intimacy to this that's unsettling in a different way than the Steppe flock.

Folk tradition describes the sensation: a "heavy breath," the feeling of being watched from just outside your field of vision. When people say "my shadow is close lately," they don't mean it metaphorically. Something is approaching that your shadow-member is preparing to witness.

The Indian Face — The Appeals Window

The specifically interesting thing about the Yamaduta tradition is that the messengers are occasionally wrong. Mistaken identity, wrong soul, wrong timing — these are folk stories across multiple traditions, heroes who visit Yama's court and are sent back because of an administrative error. This is the origin logic of the appeal process — why the Chinvat Bridge exists as a crossing rather than just an event, why Chitragupta's ledger is available for examination. The appeal exists because the system has acknowledged its own error rate.

III. Guanyu — How This Actually Works in Daily Life

His full epithet is Guanyu Shèngdì — "Guanyu the Holy Sovereign" — but nobody uses that outside formal contexts. In everyday speech he's "the Holy Lord" or "the Red General," and in merchant quarters you'll hear the shorthand "under his eye."

The Everyday Mechanics

The single most important thing about Guanyu's practical cult: no priest is required for the standard transaction. You burn a stick of incense, you state the terms of your agreement aloud into the smoke, you clap twice. Done. The incense carries the terms. This is why Guanyu is the most commercially ubiquitous deity in the eastern sphere — the entry cost is a stick of incense. Every caravanserai has a small shrine. Every traveling merchant has a portable altar. The terms of every significant deal made in this setting exist somewhere in a register that can, at least theoretically, be appealed to.

Households keep a Guanyu tablet above the front door — often just a red card with his name. When a family member makes a serious promise, they touch the tablet. When someone is accused of breaking faith, the tablet comes out. There's a reason people describe being unable to lie while touching it: the gesture isn't purely symbolic, and the social stakes of lying in front of it are real enough that the psychological mechanism works.

The military usage is worth noting separately because it reframes what "protection" means in this theology. When a soldier says "Guanyu hù wǒ" — "Guanyu protect me" — the protection they're asking for is not safety from death. They're not asking not to die. They're asking not to die badly. Not to break. Not to run. Not to fail their unit. Dying is acceptable. Dying in dishonor is what you're asking him to prevent. This is a genuinely different theological relationship with death than most fantasy settings offer.

The Spoken Idioms (These Matter for NPC Voice)

PhraseWhat It Actually Means
"By Guanyu's beard"Mild — emphasis, nothing more. "I swear to god" in casual speech.
"Guanyu's open hand"Substantive. The gesture matters: extend your palm, face up, nothing hidden. Merchants who've been doing business in the eastern sphere for years do this reflexively.
"Under the Red General's eye"Officially sworn, binding, on record. Use this when you want the other party to understand this isn't casual.
"His sword is slow but it always arrives"Proverb about consequence. The justice isn't fast — you can break an oath and live comfortably for years. But it comes. This is genuinely believed, not just rhetorically deployed.
"I can't close my hand on that"The refusal. The closed fist is the inverted Guanyu palm — you're not offering his hand, you can't swear to it.

When Trust Breaks — The Formal Challenge

If you're accused of something, you can extend your open palm and say "Guanyu's palm." This is not casual. The social weight of falsely offering Guanyu's palm is enormous, which is why the other party doesn't automatically accept it — accepting creates obligation on both sides. You're saying you believe them, which means if the accusation turns out to be true, you've put yourself in a position of complicity. People hesitate.

The Temple Economy — And Why It Matters Politically

The temple locations tell you everything: always commercial, always near the market quarter or beside the guild hall or at the trading post entrance. Always bright red. The interior architecture is worth understanding: the central Guanyu statue is flanked by a military advisor on one side and an accounting scribe on the other. These three together are the complete argument — righteous power, strategic wisdom, accurate record-keeping. The deity is that combination.

The priesthood's income streams: registration fees scale to transaction value, which means the temple register for a major trading city is the commercial history of that city. Arbitration fees mean the temple has an interest in resolving disputes rather than prolonging them. The certification seal is a functioning credit rating.

The political implication almost writes itself: the temple master of a major Guanyu temple holds the commercial records of the city's entire merchant class. They never appear to exercise power. They don't need to. The most sophisticated ones are quietly among the most influential people in their cities, and they got there entirely through the mechanics of accurate record-keeping. That's the kind of power that survives regime change.

IV. Chitragupta — The Scribe Who Sees Everything

His full epithet is Chitragupta Dharmācārya — "Chitragupta, Master of Right-Record." In everyday speech: "the Scribe," "the Accountant," "the One Who Writes."

The Difference From Guanyu

This is worth being precise about. Guanyu records agreements — what you promised. Chitragupta records what happened — the complete ledger of your deeds, all of them, in full fidelity. The relationship with Guanyu is transactional: you make a deal, you invoke him, he holds the record. The relationship with Chitragupta is existential: he's already writing, he started when you were born, and there's no opt-in moment. You don't invoke Chitragupta to enter the record. You're already in it.

This changes the practical cult completely. Chitragupta worship is less about transactional invocation and more about confession — but not for absolution. You're not confessing for forgiveness. You're confessing for accurate record. There's a meaningful difference. The act of acknowledging what you did, placing a folded paper at a roadside marker, saying "the Scribe knows I did this deliberately" — that's not asking for mercy. That's making sure the record shows you knew what you were doing. There are traditions that find this more dignified than pretending otherwise.

The Folk Phrases (and Why "Let the Scribe Read It" Has Teeth)

PhraseWhat It Actually Means
"The Scribe has it"It's real, it happened, nothing you do now changes the record of it. Used to end arguments — not triumphantly, but with a finality that acknowledges the futility of the argument itself.
"Let the Scribe read it"Formal challenge when falsely accused. But it has teeth in both directions — you're opening the ledger to that page, and that page is in a book. Other things are on nearby pages.
"Don't make me open that page"Warning: the investigation won't stop at the accusation — it'll expand until the truth is visible. Use when someone is pushing a dispute they should probably drop.
"The Scribe was watching"Formal invocation: I have a witness you can't argue with. Rarely used casually because the weight of it doesn't wear well.
"Born with clean ink"Said of newcomers, the young, those who've undergone restitution. Deliberately ambiguous — whether the ledger is literally clean or whether this is a culturally recognized moral fresh-start is left open. Both readings coexist.

What the Priests Are Actually Doing

Here's the honest description of the audit process: both parties appear, accusations and counter-claims are stated and recorded, and then the priest "meditates" for hours or days. This meditation period is the investigation. The priests are excellent investigators — they've been quietly gathering information since the dispute was brought to them. The "divine communion" framing is accurate in one sense: the result they announce is considered divine because it's accurate, not because it arrived through supernatural means.

Occasionally the investigation turns up something that genuinely surprises the priest. Experienced Chitragupta priests have learned not to show it. The neutrality is professional, not performative — though the performance of it has become so practiced that the distinction doesn't really matter from the outside.

The Temple — Ink, Lamps, and One Very Dark Pool

The Chitragupta temple has a specific atmosphere: quiet, administrative, organized by a filing system that outsiders cannot parse, small reading lamps burning at every surface. The central figure is not enthroned or armored. He's seated, writing. The pen never stops. The sound of the pen is the sound of the temple.

The Pool of All Record is the piece I find most interesting to think about. It's an ink-dark pool at the center of the main chamber, and folk tradition says the reflection shows you as you actually are rather than as you present. There are people who avoid looking into it. There are people who seek it out specifically to be honest with themselves in a way they can't manage otherwise.

Whether it actually shows anything supernatural is left genuinely open — "may be metaphor. May not." I think that's the right call for the table.

V. Žarokaz — The Fire That Shows You What Things Are

The name is Slavic-compound: žar (fire, heat, ardor — the internal fire of something, not just combustion) plus kaz from kazat' (to show, to tell, to reveal). "What the fire reveals." The fire doesn't judge. It just shows you what's there.

Why This Deity Works

Dwarven theology has a problem that most fantasy settings don't fully solve: dwarves are supposed to be craft-obsessed, but what does that mean theologically? Just "they have a forge god" is thin. Žarokaz is an attempt to work out what it actually means to have fire-and-craft as a spiritual practice rather than just an aesthetic.

The key move: the forge is not a tool for making things. The forge is a tool for revealing things. You put the metal in, and what comes out tells you what the metal was, what the craftsperson's skill is, what the design demanded and whether the craftsperson understood it. The fire doesn't add anything. It strips away everything that isn't essential. What survives is what was actually there.

The Triple Function

Craft-record: When a Dwarven journeyman completes their first independent work, they bring it to the shrine and hold it in firelight while a priest reads the marks. Not a blessing — an assessment. The priest says what the work reveals about the maker. This is sometimes deeply uncomfortable. The work doesn't lie, and the priest's job is to say what the work is saying.

Earned wealth: There's a specific Dwarven theological position on money that Žarokaz embodies: legitimate wealth is generational. It accumulates across multiple generations of honest craft and honest trade. A lucky find doesn't make you wealthy in the sense that Žarokaz recognizes — it makes you lucky. Wealth and luck are different things. This is why Dwarven banking houses that carry Žarokaz's mark are advertising something specific: our money came from work.

Battle-worth: A weapon accumulates a record over time. The maker's mark is there from the beginning, but the warrior adds to it — the nicks, the repairs, the specific wear-patterns that show how they actually fight rather than how they were trained to fight.

"This blade has killed seventeen times. Three of those were from behind. The maker would be ashamed." — That sentence should land differently than a simple reputation check. It's documentary.

The Idioms

PhraseWhat It Actually Means
"The forge has seen it"The Dwarven equivalent of Guanyu's palm — invoking the witness of fire-truth to your honesty. The weight comes from the fact that the forge can't be deceived.
"Žarokaz reads the mark"A threat that doesn't need elaboration. Your work will eventually be assessed. Your deeds will eventually be read.
"Three generations of fire"The highest compliment for a craft lineage. Not just that the work is good now — that the family has been doing honest work for three generations without a gap.
"Cold iron, cold truth"A weapon or tool made without honest craft cannot be trusted. By extension: a person who hasn't been through genuine assessment cannot be trusted either.

VI. The Last Voice — Soŋ Ses

The name "Remembrancer-Who-Forgets" was evocative but ultimately wrong about the core wound. Let me work through why.

The deity isn't about forgetting. Forgetting happens naturally, over time, and has a kind of dignity to it — the slow dissolution of things not held. What this deity is about is the specific violence of a lineage being cut. Not: the knowledge faded because no one kept it alive. But: the people who were keeping it alive were killed before they could finish the work of transmission. Those are completely different things.

Soŋ Ses — Turkic-derived, "Last Sound" or "Last Voice" — is better. The last voice of a teaching that never reached completion. The transmission that was interrupted.

What This Deity Is and Isn't

There's a clean line to draw here that matters for theology and for table:

  • Chitragupta records deeds — what you did, what was done to you. The ledger of events.
  • The Last Voice witnesses transmissions — what was being taught, what was in the act of being passed on, what the lineage was carrying when it was cut. Not the knowledge itself (which is gone — the Last Voice does not preserve it, this is important) but the fact of the transmission-in-progress.
The Scholars' Purge (1173 HB — twenty-six years before campaign present) created an enormous outstanding balance with this deity. Hundreds of scholars killed before they could finish teaching. The Last Voice holds all of those unfinished transmissions, not as preserved knowledge but as witnessed wounds. The knowledge is gone. The loss is on the record.

Orcish Practice — "Feed the Last Voice by Continuing"

The annual Orcish Remembrancer ritual is structured specifically to not be worship. They're not asking the Last Voice for protection or blessing. They're acknowledging what happens when the oral tradition fails — and by acknowledging it, by naming it, they're performing the act that keeps the deity's burden from growing. Every story told reduces what the Last Voice has to carry. Every name remembered is a small act of cosmological maintenance.

The ritual ends with full name, family name, homeland name spoken aloud. This is a declaration that the lineage continues — the Last Voice doesn't need to witness this particular silence yet.

"Feed the Last Voice by continuing" — you honor this deity not by praying to it but by making its function unnecessary.

Why This Matters for the Campaign

The heroes who are chasing the sources of the Scholars' Purge are, in Orcish cosmological terms, attempting to close wounds that the Last Voice has been carrying for twenty-six years. If they find what was being taught and ensure it reaches someone who can carry it forward, they are performing a cosmological act. They're not just uncovering a historical crime. They're completing transmissions that were interrupted. The Last Voice has an interest in this — not as revenge, but as completion.

This is the kind of thing that can change the flavor of an entire story arc. The heroes aren't just investigators. They're, in a specific sense, finishing what was started.

VII. Shizuka no Kagami — The Still Mirror

Shizuka no Kagami (静かな鏡): "The Still Mirror."

The design challenge here was: Elves and Orcs both have a relationship with severed transmission, but the relationships have to be completely different or the deity just duplicates. Here's the distinction that makes both interesting:

For Orcs, the wound is recent, violent, and external. Someone came and killed the teachers. The Scholars' Purge is twenty-six years ago, it has names and faces, it is an active grievance with living people who remember the teachers who died.

For Elves, the equivalent wound is ancient, chosen, and internal. No one killed the Elven scholars. The Elven scholars withdrew. They decided — over centuries, through a long process of increasing insularity — that mortal species couldn't fully understand, and they stopped teaching across species boundaries. They didn't lose their knowledge to violence. They've been in the process of voluntary severance. And because Elves are effectively immortal, this process hasn't ended — it's still happening, right now, in this campaign present.

What the Still Mirror Is

The Still Mirror is the patron of knowledge deliberately withheld until the holder died without passing it on. Not knowledge that was taken. Not knowledge that was forgotten in the usual sense. Knowledge that was kept, consciously, and is now gone anyway.

The Elven variant has no Last Voice's grief. That's the crucial difference. The Last Voice mourns — there's an ache in it, a desire for completion. The Still Mirror reflects. That's all. It shows you with complete equanimity what you have been choosing not to pass on, and it does this without accusation or comfort.

Being seen clearly, with no reaction, can be worse than being judged.

The Kagami no Mae Ritual

An Elven scholar who is aging — and for Elves, "aging" means a slow shift in what they find meaningful, the things that used to matter starting to recede — sometimes performs this ritual: speaking what they know into a reflective surface. Not recording it. Not transcribing it. Speaking it, once, as acknowledgment that it existed and is now passing. The mirror receives it. Nothing is preserved.

This is considered more dignified, in Elven tradition, than simply letting knowledge fade without acknowledgment. The ritual is also, if you follow the implications, a kind of confession: this is the shape of my withholding. The mirror shows you what it looks like.

"Being shown what I am keeping from the world, and understanding that I cannot stop keeping it." — The cannot is doing important work. It's not unwillingness anymore — it's something deeper, a structural incapacity built by centuries of withdrawal.

The Political Dimension

Elves who understand the Still Mirror tradition have a strange relationship with what happened in 1173 HB. They didn't participate in the Scholars' Purge. But their centuries of deliberate withdrawal created a world in which that knowledge was more vulnerable — there was less redundancy, fewer backup lineages. The Elves look at the Scholars' Purge and see in it a different version of the reflection the Still Mirror shows them.

An NPC Elven elder who has recently performed Kagami no Mae is in a particular state worth knowing as a GM: not guilty, not defensive, not grieving in the way a human character would grieve. Honest. Specifically honest about the weight of what they've been withholding, in a way they usually aren't. That's a distinctive register for an encounter — an Elf who is, for once, not performing equanimity about what's been lost, but genuinely carrying it.

VIII. Crow Court — Table Protocol

This one is almost entirely practical, but there's a design principle embedded in it worth naming.

The principle: the best GM signals are ones that have two layers — a surface reading available to everyone at the table, and a deeper reading available only to characters (and players) who've done the work to understand the system. Crows are just crows, if you don't know the grammar. If you know the grammar, crows are a real-time cosmological indicator.

Crow BehaviorWhat It Signals
Single crow, watchingThe appointment has been made for someone nearby — the Crow Court has decided, the record has been updated. One crow, sitting quietly, watching a specific person? A death already decided.
Two crows, sitting togetherThe accounting is being done. Deliberation in progress. Someone in the vicinity is being considered by the Court.
A flock landingThe Court is in full conference. A simple death doesn't require a flock. Something about this situation needs collective judgment — a jurisdiction dispute, unusual circumstances. Flocks are a flag that something cosmologically interesting is happening.
A flock taking flight all at onceThe decision has been made and they're departing. The departure is imminent. If players are watching this, they have very little time.
Crows that don't startleSomething is wrong with the normal order. The Death-Chorus is distracted, or disrupted, or dealing with something so far outside normal parameters that the usual automatic processes have paused. This is the one that should make players uncomfortable.

Players who've learned the grammar will read these. Players who haven't will just notice the birds. The table experience is different for each, and both are correct. That's the design working as intended.
