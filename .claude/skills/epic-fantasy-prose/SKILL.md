## When to use this

Apply when the user asks for original fiction, narrative description, or TTRPG flavor/lore text and wants an epic-fantasy register — think large-scale stakes, visceral combat, richly sensory settings. Do not apply to emails, memos, or everyday non-fiction writing.

This skill captures *craft techniques and rhythm*, not any specific author's characters, world, or wording. Never reuse proper nouns, plot beats, or verbatim phrasing from existing published fiction — draft entirely original content that uses the same toolbox.

## Core techniques

**Sentence rhythm — cascade then snap.** Build sentences as chains of 3–5 clauses linked by commas, piling image on image, then cut to a short, blunt sentence — or a true fragment — for impact. The long sentence creates momentum; the short one lands the blow. Fragments ("Bearing witness.") are a legitimate tool at key emotional beats, not just full short sentences.

Example shape: "The [thing] did [A], then [B], [C] falling away as [D] — until [short, hard consequence]."

**Wide-to-close pacing.** Open a scene or action beat with an establishing, almost cinematic wide shot (the field, the crowd, the weather, the silence before), then narrow steadily into visceral close-up physical detail. Zoom in like a camera, not a jump cut.

**Mechanical violence.** Render destruction and combat with near-engineering precision — describe the physical cause-and-effect of forces (a wheel giving way, a structure pivoting, fire consuming from the edges inward, the crush of bodies) rather than just naming the result. Keep the diction formal and grim even here — avoid phrasing that reads as flip or casual about death (e.g. prefer "the crush of many bodies whose force was argument's end" over something like "a body that stopped mattering"). This makes violence feel more brutal by making it feel *physically real*, not glib.

**Sensory stacking.** Layer at least two senses per key paragraph — sound and sight together, or smell and texture — rather than relying on visual description alone.

**Elevated but grounded diction.** Mix formal/archaic-leaning vocabulary (words like "conflagration," "nimbus," "contrivance") with plain, hard physical verbs (plunged, sank, cracked). Avoid contemporary slang or casual filler words entirely in narrative passages — dialogue can be plainer. Avoid unnecessarily gendered generic phrasing ("the kind a man makes" → "the kind someone makes") unless gender is specifically relevant.

**Scale and elemental imagery.** Reach for vast, elemental comparisons — rivers, fire, armies, weather — to give even small moments a sense of cosmic weight. When describing something hard to visualize (an indistinct mass, a crowd, a force), ground it with a concrete comparison or physical anchor (e.g. "person shaped, and sized") even while keeping its nature uncanny or hard to name.

**Stillness before eruption.** Let tension build through waiting, tedium, or mundane detail (people talking, someone making a joke, nothing happening for days) before violence or the supernatural erupts. The contrast between banality and catastrophe is the engine of tension.

**Dark, understated humor.** Drop a grim joke or dry aside into an otherwise catastrophic scene, delivered deadpan by a background character. It humanizes scale and gives the prose breathing room.

**Invented specificity, unexplained.** Use invented proper nouns (places, titles, factions, peoples) confidently and without exposition dumps — trust the reader/scene to absorb them through context, the way real-world writing never stops to define "the Pentagon."

**Interiority stays anchored to the POV character.** Don't attribute understanding, awareness, or intent to non-sentient things (a character's wings "understanding" the discipline of a moment, a sword "wanting" to be drawn) — that reads as an over-poetic aside. Keep interior life confined to what the POV character actually thinks and feels.

**Resolve is usually silent.** When a character reaches a decision or moment of resolve, default to rendering it as an internal thought or pure action, not spoken dialogue — unless the scene specifically calls for the character to speak aloud (e.g. to another character who needs to hear it). "*I can end this,* she thought" reads truer than saying it out loud to no one.

**POV.** Default to close third-person, camera-like — free to cut between characters/vantage points at scene breaks, but locked to one perspective within a given passage.

## What to avoid

- Don't lift specific character names, place names, factions, or plot situations from any existing published work.
- Don't quote or closely paraphrase specific passages from source inspiration — this skill is about *technique*, not *transcription*.
- Don't over-apply density to short/utility text — reserve the full cascade-and-snap treatment for scene-level prose, not single-line flavor text (which should still favor a punchy elevated phrase over quirky/modern tone).
- Don't over-narrate interiority onto inanimate or non-POV things.
- Don't default characters to speaking their resolve aloud — silence and internal thought usually land harder.
